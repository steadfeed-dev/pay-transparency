Hiring guide for small businesses

How to write a pay-transparent job ad

More than a dozen states and several cities now ask job ads to show real pay. Here’s what a good ad looks like, what each place asks for, and a checklist you can use before you post. No legal jargon.

Why this matters for a small business

Pay-transparency laws started with big employers in mind, but many of them reach very small businesses too. Colorado, Connecticut, Maryland and Virginia apply to employers of any size. New York applies from four employees. In New York, for example, an ad counts whether it is on a job board, your website, a social post, a flyer or an email to a group of candidates.

There is a practical reason too. Indeed says it may not show jobs without pay in places where the law asks for it, so an ad without a range can simply reach fewer people. And candidates skip ads that hide pay.

What a good ad shows

Pay range$18.50 – $21.00 per hour+ Benefitsmedical insurance, 401(k), paid time off+ Other paytips, bonus, commission

The pay range is the lowest and highest pay you honestly expect to offer a new hire for this job. Write both ends with a dollar sign, and say whether it is per hour or per year: Pay range: $62,000 – $74,000 per year. California now defines it as a good-faith estimate of what you reasonably expect to pay at hire. If pay truly doesn’t vary, one rate is fine in many places, for example New York, Washington, Minnesota and Virginia: Pay: $20.00 per hour.

Benefits are needed in Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, New Jersey, Washington and Jersey City. A general description is enough, and no dollar values are needed. “Great benefits” doesn’t describe anything, so name them.

Other pay such as tips, bonuses or commission gets a short mention in Colorado, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, New Jersey and Washington. Amounts are optional. For tipped jobs, show the base wage and say it’s tipped: $18 per hour plus tips is the example Colorado and New York give.

Colorado also asks for the application deadline and how to apply. “Open until filled” is not enough. Give a good-faith date (you can extend it later), or say applications are accepted on an ongoing basis if you always hire for the job.

Columbus, Ohio: what to do before January 1, 2027

Columbus amended its pay-equity ordinance in November 2025. From January 1, 2027, employers with 15 or more employees in Columbus must include a reasonable salary range or pay scale in job postings for work in Columbus. Asking applicants about their pay history is already off-limits there.

  • Count your Columbus employees. Under 15? The posting rule doesn’t reach you, but showing pay still helps.
  • Pick a range you could defend. The city says reasonableness depends on things like your budget, the experience you expect and the job’s responsibilities.
  • Update ads that will still be live on January 1, not just new ones.
  • Remove any question that asks for current or past pay.

Ohio has no statewide law. Cleveland’s rule (15+ employees in the city) has applied since October 27, 2025. Cincinnati and Toledo only require pay information on request after an offer, so they don’t change your ads.

Places with no posting rule today still let you show pay, and it usually helps. Canada (British Columbia, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick) and EU countries have their own rules, which this guide doesn’t cover.

Remote jobs

A remote job open to people across the US can be reached by several state laws. New York, for example, covers remote jobs that report to a New York office or supervisor, and Colorado covers remote jobs even if Coloradans are excluded. The simple approach: write the remote ad so it works everywhere. That means a pay range, a benefits line, a mention of other pay, an application deadline and how to apply.

Special cases

SituationWhat to write
Commission onlyPay: This position is paid solely on commission. New York, Maine, Vermont and Delaware say this is enough. Elsewhere the sources don’t say, so many employers add an expected earnings range.
Part commissionGive the base pay range and say commission is available. Vermont and Delaware ask you to say so.
TippedThe base wage, plus “plus tips”. Estimating tips is optional.
One fixed ratePay: $20.00 per hour. Accepted in many places; in places that ask for a minimum and maximum, show a range if pay could vary at all.
Very wide rangeIf the top is more than about twice the bottom, ask yourself whether you’d really pay both ends to a new hire. Ranges must be good-faith estimates.
“Please include your current salary”Remove it. California, Connecticut, Virginia, Cleveland, Columbus and many other places bar asking for pay history. Asking what pay someone expects is generally fine.

The 60-second checklist

  • Pay shows a dollar bottom and top (or one fixed rate), per hour or per year.
  • No “DOE”, “competitive” or “negotiable” standing in for numbers.
  • No “up to”, “from” or “+” figures.
  • A one-line benefits summary, if you hire in any of the eight places that ask for it.
  • Tips, bonus or commission mentioned, if the job has them.
  • Colorado or remote: an application deadline (or “ongoing”) and how to apply.
  • No question about current or past pay.
  • The same pay shows everywhere the ad appears, including job boards and social posts.
